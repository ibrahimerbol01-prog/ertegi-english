TITLE: Hit, Club!
LEVEL: C1
TARGETS: goose, pot, donkey, club, children, magic, trap, words, trick, gold, kindness, knucklebones
---
[1] There was once an old man who, together with his wife, scraped a living from the steppe with nothing but a handful of traps. Whatever the traps caught that day was what the two of them ate; on an empty day they went hungry. One day, checking his traps as usual, the old man found a goose beating its wings helplessly in one of them.
[2] Hunger argued one way and pity the other, and pity won. He loosened the trap and let the goose go. To his astonishment, the bird turned and spoke to him: if he came to find it once a full year had passed, he might ask for anything at all. His wife, when she heard the story, scolded him without mercy, yet he held on to the promise. When the year came round, he set out to see whether the words of a goose were worth anything.
[3] Along the way he came upon a vast herd of camels, and their herdsman told him they belonged to a rich man called Kazbai. The old man drew his own conclusion: the wealthy Kazbai and the goose he had freed must be one and the same. A shepherd boy filled in the rest. Kazbai, he said, owned three magic things, and each would obey only its own words. There was a pot that would cook any dish on command, a donkey that would shower its owner with gold, and a club that would thrash anyone it was ordered to. Kazbai received the old man with great kindness and gave him the pot. On the road home, however, he passed a group of children absorbed in a game of knucklebones, who called him in to share a meal. He went in, warning them firmly never to speak the pot's words. Hardly had his back turned when they did exactly that, feasted on whatever they pleased, and slipped an ordinary pot into the place of the magic one. When the old man brought the useless pot back to Kazbai, he was given the donkey instead, and the same children played precisely the same trick on him.
[4] Two gifts had come into his hands, and two had slipped through his fingers. A prouder man might have been too ashamed to go back a third time; a more suspicious one might have decided that the goose was mocking him. Should he give up on the promise altogether, or swallow his embarrassment and return once more?
[5] He returned, and this time Kazbai gave him the club. The knucklebones players, emboldened by two easy victories, waited only until the old man had gone indoors before calling out the words, "Hit, club!" The club sprang into the air and set about them with a will, and their laughter turned at once into howls.
[6] In no time they were pleading with him to call it off, swearing that they would hand back everything they had taken. The old man took them at their word and ordered the club to stop, and the children duly brought back the real pot and the real gold-giving donkey. With all three gifts in his possession, he set off for home, having at last got what he had come for. He had lost his gifts twice through trusting others, and he had won them back by refusing to give up on a promise.
[7] The tale says it plainly enough. Kindness, even to a bird caught in a trap, has a way of coming back to the giver. What is gained by trickery is held only until a stronger trick comes along.
